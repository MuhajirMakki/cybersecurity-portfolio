# USB Baiting Attack Vectors

## Introduction

In this activity, I analyzed the security risks of an unfamiliar USB drive found in a hospital parking lot. The exercise focused on USB baiting, where an attacker leaves a USB device in a location where someone is likely to find and use it. I looked at the information stored on the USB drive and considered how an attacker could use it against an employee or organization.

## Activity Scenario

I am part of the security team at Rhetorical Hospital. A USB drive with the hospital's logo was found in the parking lot. Instead of connecting it directly to a normal workstation, the USB drive was examined in a virtual environment to reduce the risk of affecting other systems.

The USB drive appeared to belong to Jorge Bailey, the human resource manager. It contained both personal and work-related files, including family and pet photos, a new hire letter, and an employee shift schedule.

## Contents

The USB drive contains a mixture of personal and work-related information belonging to Jorge. The personal files include family and pet photos, while the work files include a new hire letter and an employee shift schedule. Some of this information may contain personal or employee-related information that should not be publicly accessible.

## Attacker Mindset

An attacker could use the information to learn about Jorge, his coworkers, and the hospital's operations. This information could be used to create convincing phishing messages that appear to come from a coworker or someone Jorge knows. The USB drive could also have been intentionally placed to trick an employee into connecting it to a hospital computer.

## Risk Analysis

USB baiting can expose a company to malware such as ransomware, spyware, or other malicious software. Employee security awareness training can help people recognize suspicious USB drives and avoid connecting unknown devices to company computers. Antivirus and endpoint security tools should also scan removable devices for malicious files. Disabling AutoPlay on company computers provides another technical control by preventing files from running automatically.

## What I Learned

I learned that a USB drive can be a security risk even when it does not contain obvious malicious files. Personal and work information stored on the same device can give an attacker useful information for targeting an employee or organization. I also learned that unfamiliar USB devices should not be connected directly to normal company systems.

## Cybersecurity Connection

USB baiting is a form of social engineering because it relies on human curiosity and trust. Security awareness, endpoint protection, and technical restrictions can reduce the chance of an employee accidentally introducing malware into an organization's network.



## Conclusion

This activity showed how an ordinary USB drive can become an attack vector. The combination of personal information, work information, and possible malware makes unknown USB devices a security risk. Using a safe virtual environment, employee awareness, antivirus protection, and AutoPlay restrictions can help reduce the risks associated with USB baiting.
